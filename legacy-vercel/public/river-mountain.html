Field notes · § I

Some buyers choose a town. You're choosing the water.

The shoreline is part of the deed.

There's a kind of buyer the foothills quietly attract. They don't want walkable Main Street. They want a dock they can step onto with a coffee, deep water off the end of it in October, and a boat that never sees a trailer from April to Thanksgiving.

This is who we cater to on Lake Tulloch, New Melones, New Hogan, Pardee, and Camanche — and along the Stanislaus, the Mokelumne, and the Calaveras where a home sits right on the river. Some of these are gated lake communities. Some are a single riverfront parcel at the end of a gravel road. What they share is a shoreline that's part of the deed.

The transactions look different. The questions look different — dock permits, water levels, shoreline setbacks, body-contact rules. We've been writing waterfront contracts for years, and James was reading water-agency shoreline agreements for a decade before that.

Who this is for
01

The lake-weekend family

A ski boat, a swim dock, and kids who learn to slalom before they learn to drive. Tulloch or Camanche, close enough to the Bay for Friday nights.

Frontage on the Stanislaus or the Mokelumne. A swimming hole below the deck, the sound of moving water at night, a house that's the opposite of a subdivision.

04

The lakefront-estate buyer

Big water frontage, a boathouse, room for the whole family at Thanksgiving. The kind of place you don't sell — you pass it down.

Plate IV · What we'll ask before any offer

Nine questions before the listing photos.

01

Dock rights & permits

Private, shared, or none. Every reservoir agency — Tri-Dam, EBMUD, the Corps — has its own dock rules, and a permit doesn't always transfer with the deed. We confirm it in writing.

02

Water level & drawdown

The lake in the summer photos is not the lake in October. We pull the drawdown curve — some docks sit on dry mud by fall. Ask before you fall for the view.

03

Shoreline ownership

Do you own to the waterline, or does the agency own to a contour line above your lawn? The high-water mark decides what's actually yours. Half of waterfront surprises live here.

04

Body-contact rules

Camanche, Tulloch, and New Melones allow swimming. Pardee, a drinking-water reservoir, does not. If the plan is a swim dock, the lake has to allow it.

05

Flood zone & FEMA

Riverfront on the Stanislaus or Mokelumne means a base-flood elevation and, often, mandatory flood insurance. We pull the FEMA map before you write the offer.

06

HOA & lake association

Copper Cove, Poker Flat, Connor Estates each have dues, dock waitlists, and launch rules. We read the CC&Rs and the reserve study, not just the listing.

07

Septic setback from water

County code sets a minimum distance between the leach field and any shoreline or watercourse. On a tight waterfront lot, that setback can cap what you're allowed to build.

08

Fire zone & insurance

Waterfront is still foothill WUI. State Responsibility Area, Very-High Severity, Cal Fair Plan premiums. Quote the carrier before contingency removal — the number has tripled in five years.

09

Boat access & launch

Private ramp, marina slip, or HOA launch — and is it usable at low pool? A dock-out home is only as good as the water depth off the end of it in September.

A note from your agents · § VII
JBJames Beyersdorf · Associate · Waterfront & land specialist

A few words on buying on the water —

I came to Calaveras for the water in 2014 and never left. Most of my buyers since have been chasing the same thing — a dock they can walk to with a coffee, a boat that stays in the slip, a shoreline that's part of the deed. Some find it on Tulloch, some on Camanche, some on a quiet stretch of the Stanislaus. The shape of the frontage matters more than the postal address.

What I tell every waterfront buyer the first time we sit down: this is not the kind of transaction you rush. The questions matter more than the listing. A dock permit. The drawdown curve. Where the high-water mark actually falls. A FEMA flood zone. Whether the lake even allows a swim dock. None of these show up in the photos. All of them decide whether the next ten years are the summer you pictured or a five-year correction.

Sara and I work these deals together. She runs marketing, negotiation, and the buyer-side of the close. I do the technical walk — the shoreline, the dock, the water level, the flood map. The combination is why we win these. The combination is also why we say no to some of them.

— James
