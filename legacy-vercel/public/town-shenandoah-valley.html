Field notes · § I

Not a town.
A valley with a road through it.

Shenandoah Valley is the rarest thing in California wine: a serious appellation that never got discovered, never got priced out, and never stopped being farmland.

Gold miners planted the first vines here in the 1850s to keep themselves supplied. When the gold went, the vines stayed — and because Amador was too remote to be worth ripping out during Prohibition, some of those plantings are still in the ground. The Original Grandpère Vineyard, off Steiner Road, has been producing Zinfandel since 1869. It is the oldest documented Zinfandel planting in the United States.

What you buy here is not a Main Street. It's a ridge, a draw, a stand of blue oaks, and a view down a valley where every third driveway ends at a crush pad. Plymouth — pop. 1,073, two stoplights' worth of town — is the front door. Fiddletown is the back one. Everything between is granite, decomposed granite, and a hundred and seventy years of somebody's grandfather being right about where to plant.

Who the Valley is for —
01

The planter

Ten to forty acres, decomposed granite, a well with real numbers behind it. Barbera in the ground by the second spring. Bonded by year five, or never — both are fine here.

02

The Sacramento escapee

Fifty minutes from Fair Oaks, and the commute runs the wrong way against traffic. Keeps the Sacramento job, trades the cul-de-sac for a ridge.

03

The hospitality buyer

Looking for the guest cottage, the event lawn, the barn with power to it. Amador's short-term-rental market runs hot every harvest and every wedding season.

04

The land holder

Buying acreage with water, an ag exemption, and a fence line that predates the county. Not building yet. Fully intends to.

A note from your agent · § VII
SC Sara Cooper · Owner-Broker

A few words on Shenandoah Valley —

People find their way to Amador after Napa prices them out and Sonoma bores them. What surprises them is that this isn't a discount version of either. It's an older wine region than most of Napa, farmed by families who never sold, and it has never once tried to be charming at you.

Buying here is different from buying in a town. You are buying dirt, aspect, and water. I will ask you about your well before I ask you about your kitchen — because on a ten-acre parcel off Steiner or Bell Road, the well log is the property. I'll also tell you honestly which parcels have the granite that makes Zinfandel worth farming and which just have a nice view, and those are not always the same parcels.

The other thing to know: this is a real community, not a weekend one. Plymouth has a fair, a hardware store, and a volunteer fire department that will know your name. That's a feature for some buyers and a shock for others. I'd rather you find out in June than in escrow.

If the Valley is on your list, come drive it with me. We'll start at the market in Plymouth, run the road to Fiddletown, and I'll show you what's actually for sale — including the two or three parcels that never make it to the MLS.

— Sara
